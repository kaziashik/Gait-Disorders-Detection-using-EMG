Summary of Dynamic markers of altered gait rhythm in amyotrophic lateral sclerosis


This study investigated how people with Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis (ALS), a condition affecting the nerves that control muscles, walk differently compared to healthy individuals. The researchers hypothesized that individuals with ALS would display less steady and more unpredictable walking patterns. To test this, they analyzed the walking patterns of ALS patients and compared them to healthy individuals and those with other conditions like Parkinson's and Huntington's diseases, which affect the basal ganglia part of the brain. Participants wore ankle-worn devices to record step duration and variation. The results confirmed that people with ALS indeed exhibited less stable and less organized walking patterns compared to healthy individuals. Additionally, the study revealed some commonalities and differences in the way ALS, Huntington's, and Parkinson's diseases affected walking. These findings suggest that monitoring the dynamics of walking could help understand and track the progression of these diseases and the effectiveness of treatments.


researchers looked at individuals with Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis (ALS) who were able to walk for at least 5 minutes without assistance and did not have other conditions that could affect their walking. They recruited eleven participants with ALS, and their ages ranged from 36 to 70 years old. The time since their ALS diagnosis varied, with an average of 21.3 months. Some participants had familial ALS. Their medication use remained unchanged, and they all provided informed consent for the study, which was approved by the Massachusetts General Hospital Institutional Review Board.

The researchers compared the data from the ALS group with data from previous studies on subjects with Huntington's disease (HD), Parkinson's disease (PD), and healthy individuals. The ALS and PD groups were, on average, older than the other groups. Most of the healthy individuals and those with HD were women, while most of the PD subjects were men.

The heights and weights of the participants in all groups were similar and did not significantly impact the measures of their walking patterns. The researchers also assessed the presence of clinical symptoms that could affect how the participants walked, like weakness in their lower extremities, without knowing the gait data beforehand.
